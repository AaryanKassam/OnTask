# The research behind OnTask

Each feature below comes from a study or review on attention, ADHD or self-regulation. The same text appears in the phone app under **Settings → Why it works**.

## Phone out of sight

People did measurably worse on working-memory tests with their phone on the desk than with it in another room, even when it was face down and switched off.

**How to use it:** Start a session, then put the phone out of reach. OnTask keeps time and chimes at each break, so you never need to look at it.

Source: [Ward et al., 2017, “Brain Drain”](https://doi.org/10.1086/691462)

## If-then plans

Deciding in advance how you will respond to a distraction had a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals across 94 studies.

**How to use it:** Fill in “If I get distracted, I will…” before you start. OnTask shows your plan when you come back from your phone.

Source: [Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1)

## Random check-ins

Students with ADHD who regularly recorded whether they were paying attention showed less off-task behaviour in class.

**How to use it:** Turn on check-ins in Settings. A chime asks “Were you on task?” at unpredictable times, and your answers feed the Insights tab.

Source: [Mirnasab & Ghobari Bonab, 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3395940/)

## Park a thought

Unfinished goals keep interrupting your thinking until you make a plan for them, even when you don’t act on that plan yet.

**How to use it:** When something pops into your head mid-block, tap Park a thought and write it down. Review the list on your break.

Source: [Masicampo & Baumeister, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024192)

## Where you left off

Part of your attention stays on an unfinished task after you switch away, which hurts whatever you do next.

**How to use it:** At the start of each break, jot down your exact next step. OnTask shows it when the break ends, so you can let go during the break and restart quickly.

Source: [Leroy, 2009, “attention residue”](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2009.04.002)

## Regular short breaks

Focus on a long task fades over time, and brief, planned breaks in the task largely prevented that drop.

**How to use it:** Pick a study method. The Insights tab suggests shorter or longer blocks based on when you actually tend to drift.

Source: [Ariga & Lleras, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21211793/)

## Movement breaks

Across studies of children and teens with ADHD, exercise improved executive function, with moderate-to-large effects on impulse control and mental flexibility.

**How to use it:** Every break suggests something active to do. A few minutes of movement before the next block beats scrolling.

Source: [Liang et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12966-021-01135-6)

## Time you can see

Adults with ADHD often perceive time differently, and researchers argue this is a core part of the condition rather than a side effect.

**How to use it:** The ring shrinks as the block runs out, and your first tiny step is shown at the start, so “later” stays visible as “now”.

Source: [Weissenberger et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8293837/)

## Ignoring the noise

Both the extension and the app treat short trips away as noise rather than drift: a glance at a message is not the same as twenty minutes on it. The extension waits for 2 minutes off task before prompting; the app ignores trips away under 15 seconds and asks you about longer ones instead of assuming. Both settings are adjustable.
